Raíces / Judhur

Personal project  ·  Complete  ·  SolidWorks, MJF, washi

The finished lamp, lit

I’m from Zaragoza on my mother’s side and from Bethlehem on my father’s. Growing up, I saw the Mudéjar star in Zaragoza’s old churches, like La Seo, built where the city’s great mosque once stood. Mudéjar art carried Islamic geometry into Christian buildings, two of my cultures meeting in one pattern. When my mom gave me a silver pendant of the star and told me its history, it felt like I could finally hold on to one thing that encompassed more than just half of who I was.

Raíces and Judhur both mean roots, in Spanish and in Arabic. This lamp is my way of making my room mine, carefully and intentionally.

A lamp built from a 3D-printed nylon lattice lined with kozo washi. The canopy carries an eight-pointed Mudéjar star lattice, based on the open-centered outline stars of Spanish alicatado tilework. I modeled the whole skeleton in SolidWorks as a single printable body, had it printed in one piece by multi jet fusion, and lined it by hand with washi sized with agar. Lit from inside, the stars read as a dark, crisp silhouette against glowing paper.

The lamp was designed as a pendant. For now, it sits on a borrowed table base.

Phase 1

Designing the skeleton

From Truss to Star

The first lattice used hoops, verticals, and crossing diagonals through shared nodes. When I built two bands and looked at them, they read as a structural truss, not Mudéjar ornament: all four line families met at single points, so there was no open star center. I suppressed that version rather than deleting it and redesigned around the alicatado motif, reusing the rings, axis, station planes, and node sketches.

Each cell between two rings now holds one eight-pointed outline star. Neighboring stars meet tip to tip, and the gaps between them form the crosses of the classic star-and-cross layout: 64 stars across four bands, from the seam ring up to a crown ring at the oculus.

Front view of the skeleton

Isometric view, with the hub and ribs below the seam ring

A side effect I kept: because each star lies on a flat plane through its cell corners, every band becomes a 16-sided faceted lantern. That faceting is itself characteristic of Mudéjar lanterns and cupolas.

Phase 2

Making it

Fabrication

After a round of test fits, I 3D printed the skeleton in one piece in multi jet fusion (MJF) nylon. MJF needs no support structures, so the dome printed without splitting at the widest ring, and it gave crisper star outlines and a tougher part than an FDM print would have.

The skeleton as printed, in raw grey MJF nylon

Skinning the Lattice

The skin went through the same kind of rethinking as the lattice. My first plan was a cast agar bioplastic sheet draped over the skeleton while leathery. I replaced it with thin kozo washi, a Japanese mulberry paper, sized onto the struts with a dilute agar solution (about 1–1.5% agar with a few drops of glycerin), tinted with black tea. The paper keeps the lightness of the lattice, the agar keeps the bioplastic idea in the bond, and the tea gives the glow a warm, honeyed tone.

Testing before committing

Before skinning the whole dome, I sized single washi squares onto individual star cells to compare three variables: paper on the outside of the lattice, paper on the inside, and size tinted with black tea for a warmer glow. Outside, the struts showed through as soft shadow lines. Inside, they stood in front of the paper as a crisp, dark star silhouette. I chose the inside, because it makes the Mudéjar pattern the main event, and used the tea-tinted size for the whole skin.

Test cells on the middle band

From gores to bands

I planned 16 vertical gores, one per star column, and tested a single full-height strip. It bonded well, but its edge ran through the open part of a cell, and when lit every overlap shows as a darker line where two layers of paper meet. So I switched to horizontal bands. A sphere is curved in two directions and won't take a flat sheet, but each band between two rings curves in only one direction, so a single strip wraps it cleanly. The horizontal seams land on the rings, where they disappear, and each band needs only one vertical seam at the back.

The first horizontal band

The final print has no binding grooves, so the band edges are folded over the rings and sized down instead of being bound with thread.

Patchwork as a material choice: where the paper opened up or creased, I patched it with more washi rather than starting over. The layered patches, visible fibers, and uneven translucency give the lamp a raw, handmade surface, and the star lattice carries the order, so the paper doesn't have to.

Appendix

Technical notes

Canopy Form

The canopy is a lofted surface through five profile circles tied to a 3-point arc silhouette, giving a near-spherical ovoid: seam ring r = 2.60″ at Z = 2.5″, widest r = 3.17″ at Z = 5.16″, oculus r = 1.25″ at Z = 8.00″.

A deliberate trade-off: a traditional Mudéjar bell needs an inflection point, which a circular arc cannot produce; it would take a spline. I chose to keep the near-spherical shape and keep the project moving, rather than rework the silhouette for precision the lamp doesn't need.

Building the Lattice from Nodes

My first plan was to project a flat lattice sketch onto the dome, and it kept failing. Parts of the sketch fell outside the face along the projection direction, and below the widest ring a straight projection hits the doubly curved surface twice. Instead of fighting the surface, I switched to a method that doesn't depend on it.

I set up a reference axis on the dome centerline and station planes every 22.5°, which divides the dome into 16 segments. Every structural node is fully defined by two relations: it sits on a profile circle and on a station plane. I modeled a single 22.5° repeat unit and generated the rest with a 16× circular pattern, which keeps the model parametric and replaced roughly 100 hand-placed struts with a few lines per band.

Star Construction

Each star is a parametric sketch on a plane through three of its cell's corner nodes: a diamond through the edge midpoints, overlaid with a rectangle scaled by 1/√2 using sketch equations, trimmed to one closed 16-segment outline. Each star is swept as a strut and patterned 16 times around its band.

Problems I Solved

Getting the lattice to combine into one solid took a series of fixes, and I traced each failure to its cause rather than patching it.

Zero-thickness contacts. Combine failed where the seam ring met the strut ends, because the ring had been extruded in one direction and its face sat coplanar with the struts. A mid-plane extrusion fixed it. Neighboring stars touching exactly at their tips caused the same error, so I added 0.14″ spherical node beads at every shared tip. They give each joint real shared volume and read as a rivet-style detail. I validated the beads on one band before applying them to the rest.

Spikes at the crown. Sweep mitering at the acute star tips left spikes poking above the crown ring. Instead of cutting them off afterward, I flattened those tips in the sketch so each ends 0.06″ below the point inside the ring.

Sketches on tilted planes. The standard center rectangle aligns to the sketch axes, not the star, so it broke on the tilted cell planes. A three-point center rectangle aligned to the star's own axes solved it.

Proportions. The first stars looked too heavy for their size, so I moved from round 0.11″ struts to flat-faced struts with crisper outlines. I also managed the bodies deliberately, separating sweeps that had merged into a rib body and rejoining everything with body patterns and Combine.
